Finding an old news article, a critical blog post, or an outdated court record when you Google your own name is unsettling, and it's a problem a lot of people quietly deal with — a bad business review that went viral, a years-old mugshot site, a news story about something that's since been resolved. This guide separates what you can realistically do from what no one — including paid reputation firms — can actually guarantee.

## First: Understand What "Negative" Means to Google

Google's ranking systems aren't making moral judgments about content; they're ranking pages by relevance, authority, and freshness signals for a given search query. A negative result ranks highly because it's well-linked, frequently referenced, or simply older and more established than anything else connected to your name — not because Google is "targeting" you.

That distinction matters because it tells you the actual lever available: **you generally can't force Google to take down legitimate, legally published content just because it's unflattering.** What you *can* do is work within Google's actual removal policies for a narrower set of cases, and build a stronger set of competing, legitimate pages for everything else.

## Step 1: Check Whether It Qualifies for Direct Removal

Before anything else, confirm whether the content falls into one of Google's actual removal categories — covered in detail in our guide on [how to remove personal information from Google](/privacy-guides/how-to-remove-personal-information-from-google/). This mainly covers exposed personal contact information, non-consensual imagery, and similar sensitive categories — not general negative coverage.

If the content is a **mugshot or arrest record site**, some of these operate on a pay-to-remove model, which several states have made illegal; even where it isn't, a direct removal request citing the case outcome (dismissal, expungement) is worth sending before considering anything else.

If the content was published with factual errors, contacting the publisher directly to request a correction is usually faster and more durable than any indirect strategy.

## Step 2: Check If the Underlying Record Can Be Changed

For some negative results — an old court case, for example — the real fix is at the source:

- **Expungement or record sealing**, where legally available, can remove the public record itself, which in turn removes what data brokers and court-record aggregators can republish. Rules vary significantly by state and offense type, so this is a question for a licensed attorney in your state, not something to attempt from general guidance.
- **Business or review platform disputes**, for a false or policy-violating review, go through that platform's own reporting process — see our guide on [handling negative reviews](/online-reviews/).

## Step 3: Build Genuine, Competing Content

This is the legitimate core of what's often marketed as "reputation repair," and it's worth understanding honestly: it is not about hiding anything. It's about giving Google — and the people searching your name — more accurate, current, and substantial information to rank alongside or above an old result. Over time, as newer, more actively linked pages about you accumulate authority, they tend to rank for name searches alongside or ahead of older content; an old result less frequently updated or linked naturally has less to compete with.

Legitimate approaches:

- A **personal website or portfolio** using your real name as the domain or a prominent page title
- An active, complete **LinkedIn profile**
- Guest posts, interviews, or contributions on relevant, reputable sites in your field
- An active, consistent presence on one or two platforms relevant to your work, rather than a dozen abandoned profiles

See our more detailed walkthrough in [how to improve Google search results for your name](/privacy-guides/how-to-improve-google-search-results-for-your-name/).

## Step 4: Be Realistic About Timelines and Limits

A few honest expectations:

- **This takes months, not days.** Competing content needs time to be indexed, linked to, and to accumulate the signals that affect ranking.
- **Nothing guarantees a #1 position change or that an old page disappears from page one.** Any company promising a guaranteed outcome is making a claim no one can actually back — Google's ranking algorithm isn't something any outside party controls.
- **Some content simply won't move**, particularly major news coverage from authoritative outlets. In those cases, the realistic goal shifts from "remove it" to "make it proportionate" — ensuring it's one data point among many accurate, current ones, not the only thing visible.

## When to Consider Professional Help

A reputation management service can be worth it if the situation involves many search results, requires sustained content production over time, or if you simply don't have the time to do the legitimate groundwork yourself. If you do explore paid help, our guide on [choosing a business reputation management service](/business-reputation/) covers what distinguishes a legitimate provider from one making unrealistic promises — the core test is the same: no one can guarantee specific ranking positions or guaranteed removals, and any pitch that does is a red flag regardless of price.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I pay to have Google remove a negative article about me?**
No legitimate path allows paying to remove legally published content that doesn't fall under Google's sensitive-information removal policy. Be skeptical of anyone claiming otherwise.

**How long does it take to push down a negative search result?**
There's no fixed timeline — it depends on the authority of the negative result and how much genuine, linked content you build. Many people see meaningful movement over several months of consistent effort, not weeks.

**Does deleting my social media accounts help?**
Not usually, and it can backfire — active, legitimate profiles are often part of what competes favorably for name searches. Deleting accounts just removes potential positive signals without affecting the negative one.
