If you've ever typed your own name into Google and found your home address, phone number, or other personal details sitting in the results, you already understand the problem this guide addresses. The good news: Google gives you several legitimate ways to request removal of specific types of personal information, and understanding how they work puts you back in control of what a search for your name turns up.

This guide walks through exactly what Google will and won't remove, how to submit a request correctly, and what to do when the information lives on a website Google doesn't control.

## What Google Will Actually Remove

Google draws a clear line between information that's merely *unflattering* and information that's genuinely *risky to have exposed*. Its [removal policies](https://support.google.com/websearch/troubleshooter/9685456) cover a specific list of personal-information categories, including:

- Government ID numbers (Social Security, passport, national ID)
- Bank account and financial account numbers
- Images of handwritten signatures
- Medical records
- Personal contact information (home address, phone number, personal email) when shared with an intent to harass, intimidate, or threaten, or alongside explicit or implicit threats
- Non-consensual explicit imagery
- "Doxxing" content that exposes personal information alongside threats

Google generally does **not** remove public-record information such as court filings, business registrations, or information you voluntarily published yourself unless it falls into one of the sensitive categories above. It also won't delist a page simply because the content is unflattering, embarrassing, or something you'd rather not have discovered — that falls under free-expression and public-interest considerations, not the removal policy.

## Step 1: Find Exactly Where the Information Appears

Before filing anything, search your own name in quotes, plus variations:

- `"Your Full Name"`
- `"Your Full Name" phone number`
- `"Your Full Name" address`
- `"Your Full Name" city state`

Note the exact URL of each page where sensitive information appears — Google's removal form asks for specific URLs, not just a general complaint. A reverse-image search on any photos tied to your name is also worth doing if you're concerned about images.

## Step 2: Use Google's "Results About You" Tool

Google now offers a dedicated [Results about you](https://myactivity.google.com/results-about-you) tool that lets you set up alerts for new search results containing your contact information, and request removal directly from the same dashboard. This is the fastest path for phone numbers, home addresses, and email addresses that meet the policy criteria.

1. Sign in with your Google Account and open Results about you.
2. Run a search using your name and other identifying details.
3. For any result containing your personal information, select **Remove result**, then follow the prompts to describe why it qualifies.
4. Submit and monitor your email for Google's decision.

## Step 3: Use the Legacy Removal Request Form (If Needed)

For categories not covered by Results about you — such as non-consensual imagery, financial account numbers, or doxxing content — use the [general removal request form](https://support.google.com/websearch/troubleshooter/9685456). You'll need to:

- Select the category that matches your situation
- Provide the exact URL(s)
- Explain, specifically, why the content meets Google's policy (vague requests are far more likely to be denied)
- Provide your contact email for follow-up

Google typically reviews requests within several days to a few weeks, though complex cases can take longer.

## Step 4: Understand What Removal From Google Actually Does

This is the detail most people miss: when Google "removes" a result, it delists the page from *Google Search* — it does not delete the page from the website hosting it. If a data broker site published your address, Google removal stops that specific listing from showing up in Google's results, but the page itself is still live, and it may still show up in Bing, DuckDuckGo, or if someone visits the site directly.

If your real goal is getting the information off the internet entirely, you need a second step: contacting the website itself.

## Step 5: Go After the Source — Contact the Website Directly

For data broker and people-search sites specifically, most maintain an opt-out process, often required by state privacy laws (California's CCPA, for example, gives residents a right to request deletion from many data brokers). Look for a page titled "Opt Out," "Do Not Sell My Info," or "Privacy Choices," usually linked in the site footer.

For content published by an individual or a smaller website, a polite, direct removal request to the site owner or webmaster — explaining what's exposed and why — resolves a surprising number of cases without any legal escalation.

## What to Expect Realistically

A few honest caveats worth setting expectations around:

- **Removal isn't instant.** Even approved requests can take days to propagate through Google's index.
- **New listings can reappear.** Data brokers continuously re-scrape public records, so a removed listing can resurface months later from a different source. Ongoing monitoring, not a one-time fix, is the realistic approach.
- **Not everything qualifies.** If the information is a matter of public record (property records, court documents) and doesn't meet one of Google's sensitive-category triggers, delisting is unlikely — your more effective lever is often the source record itself or search-result suppression through other legitimate content.
- **No service can guarantee removal.** Be skeptical of any company, including reputation-management firms, that promises guaranteed removal or a specific ranking outcome — no one controls Google's index that completely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does removing a result from Google delete the original webpage?**
No. Google removal only affects what appears in Google Search results. The underlying page remains live unless you separately contact the hosting website.

**How long does Google take to process a removal request?**
Timelines vary, but many requests are reviewed within a few days to a few weeks, depending on category and volume.

**Can I remove negative news articles about myself?**
Generally no — Google does not remove legitimate news coverage simply because it's unflattering. See our guide on [dealing with negative search results](/privacy-guides/how-to-deal-with-negative-search-results/) for the realistic options in that situation.

**Is there a cost to request removal through Google?**
No, Google's removal tools are free. Be cautious of third parties charging large fees for something you can do yourself directly with Google.
